# The Schneider Case: 5 Strategic Marketing Lessons Every Jewelry Store Can Apply

> *"What matters is the beer."* With that apparently simple line, a forgotten brand in Buenos Aires went from 2.1% to 4.1% market share in just two months, in one of the most concentrated and competitive sectors in Argentina.

In jewelry retail, something very similar happens to what the Argentinian beer market experienced in the early 2000s: a few large players concentrate most of the volume, international chains push hard with mass marketing, and independent jewelry stores —even those with excellent products— struggle to be heard.

The **Schneider case** (CCU, 2001) is one of the most studied examples of how a secondary brand, with a limited budget, manages to break the inertia of the market. And although we are talking about beer, the lessons are **directly transferable** to any jewelry store that wants to compete with large chains in a mature market.

In this article we analyze the five key lessons of the case and bring them down to the jewelry sector, also showing how a specialized ERP like **RayTecno/RayGold** turns each of those lessons into something measurable and operational.

---

## The context: a concentrated and mature market

Before getting into the lessons, it is worth understanding the scenario. In 2001, the Argentinian beer market was dominated by:

- **Quilmes** — ~65% market share
- **Brahma** — ~16%
- **CCU** (owner of Schneider) — ~11%
- **Isenbeck** — ~7%

Schneider was a brand with tradition but **without clear positioning** in Buenos Aires city and Greater Buenos Aires, the highest-volume areas in the country. CCU could not afford a price war nor an advertising investment war against Quilmes or Brahma.

Sound familiar? Swap "brewery" for "independent jewelry store" and "Quilmes" for "multinational chain with a presence in shopping centers", and you have the competitive map of much of European and Latin American jewelry retail.

---

## Lesson 1 — Segment before attacking

Schneider **did not try to steal customers from Quilmes**. It identified a specific segment —Brahma's, made up of rational consumers who valued a recognized brand + standard quality + accessible price— and designed its entire strategy for that profile.

### Application to the jewelry sector

Most independent jewelry stores make the opposite mistake: they try to be "for everyone". The result: neither the bride looking for an engagement ring, nor the executive buying a mid-range watch as a gift, nor the recurring customer restocking costume jewelry feels truly identified.

**How an ERP enables this:**

An ERP like RayTecno allows you to segment your customer base by:

- Average ticket and purchase frequency
- Preferred product families (gold, silver, diamond, pearl, in-house workshop…)
- Origin channel (physical store, web, wholesale, B2B)
- Real margin generated per customer

Without this data, "segmenting" is an exercise in intuition. With it, it is an evidence-based decision.

## Lesson 2 — Position yourself against the sector's stereotypes

When the Diálogo agency analyzed the beer market, it detected two clichés repeated by every brand: **foreign origin** ("German, Dutch, American beer…") and **low price as a synonym of inferior quality**.

Schneider did exactly the opposite: it was domestic, of good quality, and accessibly priced. Instead of hiding it, the brand turned it into its banner with the slogan *"What matters is the beer."* A phrase that, read today, seems obvious. At the time, it was **disruptive** because it broke the rulebook.

### Application to the jewelry sector

The jewelry sector is saturated with the same clichés:

- "Italian design"
- "Centuries-old artisanal tradition"
- "Affordable luxury jewelry"
- Black-and-white images of a goldsmith's hands

A differential positioning can be built from the opposite end: real gold traceability, total transparency on margins, personalized service with customer data, or a clear proposition of **objective value** ("what matters is the piece, not the packaging").

**How an ERP enables this:**

Traceability —from the ingot to the sold piece— is only possible with a system that controls manufacturing, stock, lots and sales in an integrated way. In other words, **"transparency" as positioning is not a slogan: it is an operational capability** that the ERP enables.

## Lesson 3 — Focus on a single medium (or a single channel) to maximize impact

Schneider did not spread itself thin. It chose **a single medium**: radio. And within radio, a single recognizable voice: Flavio Pedemonti, an underground character with his own identity. Result: 750,000 pesos of investment, 70 different pieces, a single idea hammered home to useful saturation.

The temptation to "be everywhere" (TV, radio, outdoor, social media, influencers) is what dilutes small budgets and makes them invisible.

### Application to the jewelry sector

A jewelry store with a limited marketing budget must choose **a channel where it can dominate**, not a symbolic presence everywhere. Some options by profile:

- **Bridal jewelry** → Instagram + collaboration with local wedding planners
- **Investment / gold jewelry** → Technical SEO + educational content
- **Corporate B2B jewelry** → LinkedIn + segmented email marketing
- **Neighborhood / loyalty jewelry** → WhatsApp Business + CRM with purchase history

**How an ERP enables this:**

Without customer data, there is no direct channel. An ERP with a CRM module lets you build segmented lists (birthdays, purchase anniversaries, products of interest) that feed email or WhatsApp campaigns with conversion rates far above mass marketing.

## Lesson 4 — Build a brand imaginary, not just a product description

The Schneider campaign did not list alcohol percentages or talk about hop quality. It built **an imaginary**: the character, the irreverent tone, the repeated tagline. The brand went from being "just another beer on the shelf" to having **recognizable character**.

### Application to the jewelry sector

A jewelry store without an imaginary is a display case with pieces. A jewelry store with an imaginary is an experience the customer wants to repeat and recommend.

The imaginary is built with:

- Visual consistency (web, packaging, store, social media)
- Recognizable editorial tone
- Sales rituals (how a piece is delivered, what story accompanies it)
- Memorable after-sales service (free annual cleaning, clear warranty, reminders)

**How an ERP enables this:**

Consistent after-sales service —the detail that builds the most loyalty— is impossible without a system. Remembering that a customer bought her ring exactly one year ago, sending her a personalized message and offering free cleaning is an action that **only scales if the ERP automates it**.

## Lesson 5 — Measure results with concrete KPIs, not gut feelings

The Schneider team knew exactly what had happened:

- September 2001: **2.1%** market share
- November 2001: **4.1%** market share
- Third place in supermarket sales in 90 days
- Silver Mercury Award from the Argentinian Marketing Association

It was not "a good campaign". It was a campaign with **KPIs measured before, during and after**.

### Application to the jewelry sector

Too many jewelry stores evaluate campaigns with phrases like "we sold more this month" or "I noticed more people in the store". Without KPIs, no learning is possible.

Minimum KPIs every jewelry store should measure:

- Average ticket by product family
- Conversion rate (visits → sales)
- Repurchase frequency per customer
- Real margin per line (not turnover)
- Stock rotation by family
- Cost of acquiring a new customer vs value of a recurring customer

**How an ERP enables this:**

These indicators are not calculated in a spreadsheet at the end of the month: they are monitored **in real time** from the ERP dashboard. It is the difference between driving while looking in the rear-view mirror and driving while looking through the windshield.

The Schneider case did not work because they had a bigger budget, a better product or a longer history. It worked because **they combined a clear strategy with measurable execution**. That formula is perfectly replicable in the jewelry sector —in fact, it is the only realistic way an independent jewelry store can compete with large chains in the medium term.

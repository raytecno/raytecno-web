# AI in jewelry manufacturing systems and quality systems

> *In 1986, W. Edwards Deming published* Out of the Crisis *with 14 principles to lift American industry out of its quality decline. Forty years later, AI is taking over many of the functions Deming assigned to statistical inspection, continuous improvement and process control. The reasonable question is: are the 14 points still valid? Or has AI rewritten them?*
>
> *The short answer: most are still valid. Some have been reformulated. Others have emerged outside the original framework. And AI, far from replacing Deming, is —when done right— the best technical vehicle that has ever existed to implement his philosophy.*

In the jewelry sector, where a piece with a loose setting or an irregular plating destroys brand reputation faster than any marketing campaign, quality **is not an optional attribute**: it is a survival condition. In this article we cross three dimensions:

1. **Deming's 14 points** and their real validity in 2026.
2. The capabilities of **AI in manufacturing and quality control** today.
3. How a **flexible manufacturing system (FMS)** integrated into a modern jewelry ERP like [RayGold](/en) materializes what in 1986 was almost philosophy.

---

## 1. Why Deming is still the right starting point

Before talking about AI, it is worth recalling **Deming's chain**: improve quality → costs go down (less rework, less scrap, fewer returns) → productivity increases → you capture more market → you generate employment and remain in business. This sequence, formulated four decades ago, **still describes precisely** what separates jewelry businesses that grow from those that languish.

What has changed is not the logic. It is **the amount of data available, the speed at which it is processed and the precision with which one acts on it**. And that is where AI comes in.

---

## 2. Deming's 14 points in 2026: what remains valid and what has changed

Let's review the 14 points one by one, applied to the jewelry workshop, comparing the original formulation with what AI and modern systems contribute today.

### Point 1 · Create constancy of purpose for product improvement

**Deming (1986):** management must have a firm long-term purpose, not quarterly strategies.

**2026:** **fully valid, possibly more than ever.** Jewelry businesses that change strategy every season destroy their ability to learn. AI does not replace this: **it reinforces it**, because machine learning models need consistent data accumulated over years to produce useful predictions. Without constancy, no clean data; without clean data, no AI worth anything.

**How the ERP makes it real:** continuous KPI traceability over years, without resets due to system changes.

### Point 2 · Adopt a new philosophy

**Deming (1986):** "commonly accepted" levels of errors and defects can no longer be tolerated.

**2026:** **valid and radicalized.** Tolerance for defects in jewelry has plummeted since 1986. Today a customer photographs the setting of their ring with a 200-megapixel mobile phone and posts it on social media if they find an imperfection. AI materializes this philosophy with **100% computer vision inspection**: every piece, every shift, same standard.

### Point 3 · Cease dependence on mass inspection

**Deming (1986):** 100% inspection to achieve quality is equivalent to planning for defects. Quality must be built into the process, not inspected at the end.

**2026:** **the most interesting point on the list.** AI introduces an important nuance. Today **computer vision inspection is so cheap, fast and precise** that it is applied to 100% of pieces without prohibitive cost. But —and this is what is Demingian— the goal of inspecting at 100% **is not to catch defects, it is to feed the model with data so that the upstream process corrects itself**. Modern inspection does not replace process improvement: it is a sensor that enables it.

General industrial sector data in 2026: computer vision systems achieve **99% detection accuracy**, inspect more than 10,000 pieces/hour at less than 100 ms per piece, and maintain the same standard 24 hours without fatigue. By contrast, a human inspector loses **20-30% accuracy after 30-40 minutes** of continuous inspection.

### Point 4 · End the practice of awarding business on price alone

**Deming (1986):** choose suppliers by quality / long-term relationship, not by price. Tend toward a single supplier per item.

**2026:** **valid in spirit, nuanced in practice.** After the pandemic, the war in Ukraine and global logistics disruptions, the single supplier doctrine is **dangerous**. Modern practice is **controlled dual sourcing**: two or three strategic suppliers per critical input (gold, certified gems, rubbers), with long relationships but with redundancy. AI helps here with **supplier scoring models** that combine price, historical quality, deadlines, LBMA/Kimberley/RJC certifications and logistics resilience.

### Point 5 · Improve constantly the system

**Deming (1986):** continuous improvement of the production and service system. It is the famous PDCA cycle (Plan-Do-Check-Act).

**2026:** **fully valid, now executable at speed impossible in 1986.** The PDCA cycle in a jewelry workshop used to be monthly or quarterly. Today with AI it is **continuous and autonomous**: the system detects a deviation in the average weight of pieces after casting, identifies that it correlates with the furnace temperature, suggests the adjustment and applies it automatically. PDCA in hours, not in months.

### Point 6 · Institute training on the job

**Deming (1986):** train people continuously, not just when they enter.

**2026:** **valid, transformed.** AI changes the nature of training in jewelry. A young setter today learns with AI assistants that show them similar pieces already produced, alerts of typical failures and step-by-step guides. The master setter remains essential —AI does not learn to set—, but the learning curve is dramatically shortened.

### Point 7 · Adopt and institute leadership

**Deming (1986):** the leader helps people and machines do their job better, doesn't control them with fear.

**2026:** **100% valid.** AI does not replace leadership. It needs it more than ever: someone has to decide which metrics to optimize, what thresholds to define as "defect", what to do when the model is wrong. Human leadership is the only thing that guarantees AI serves quality and doesn't become a black box producing meaningless numbers.

### Point 8 · Drive out fear

**Deming (1986):** no professional can give their best until they feel safe. Fear hides problems.

**2026:** **more valid than ever, and critical for AI.** Here a new risk emerges: fear that AI will detect errors that previously went unnoticed can lead operators to manipulate data. If a setter knows that every piece of theirs goes through computer vision and the error is penalized, they may start hiding them instead of learning. Demingian leadership remains the only way for AI to produce improvement and not resentment.

### Point 9 · Break down barriers between departments

**Deming (1986):** cross-departmental teamwork.

**2026:** **valid and technically easier.** An integrated ERP like RayTecno with connected modules (CAD → manufacturing → quality → sales → after-sales) eliminates information barriers. AI can correlate across departments: "pieces with this design feature have 12% more end-customer returns". Before, that correlation took months of manual analysis.

### Point 10 · Eliminate slogans, exhortations and empty targets

**Deming (1986):** "zero defects" type exhortations without method only generate frustration.

**2026:** **valid and reinforced.** AI provides the "method" that Deming demanded. "Reduce setting defects by 30%" without more is a useless slogan. "Reduce setting defects by 30% via post-setting computer vision with feedback to the setter in less than 60 seconds" is an executable program.

### Point 11 · Eliminate numerical quotas and management by objectives

**Deming (1986):** numerical quotas without method are managed with fear and degrade quality.

**2026:** **the most controversial point today.** In its absolute form it is surpassed: companies like Google work with OKRs (Objectives & Key Results) which are numerical quotas. The difference is **how they are built**. Bad Demingian quotas are arbitrary and imposed. Modern OKRs —when done well— are negotiated with method, data and continuous feedback. Deming's spirit remains correct; the literal formulation is nuanced.

### Point 12 · Remove barriers to pride of workmanship

**Deming (1986):** remove what prevents the operator from feeling proud of what they do.

**2026:** **fully valid, with new risk.** AI can boost pride (the setter sees their pieces with quality metrics and visible improvement) or destroy it (if used only as surveillance). The difference is in **leadership and communication**, not in technology.

### Point 13 · Institute a vigorous program of education

**Deming (1986):** deep training, not just operational training.

**2026:** **more urgent than ever.** The jewelry workshop of 2026 needs people who understand CAD, basic AI, dashboard reading, KPIs, in addition to the traditional craft. Training is not a course, it is a **continuous practice**.

### Point 14 · Take action to accomplish the transformation

**Deming (1986):** transformation is everyone's job.

**2026:** **100% valid.** AI and an FMS are not "implemented" from IT. They are implemented from management, with the entire team, with patience, with iteration. AI projects usually fail due to change management, not technology.

## 3. What Deming could not foresee: three layers that only exist in 2026

There are three dimensions that in 1986 simply **did not exist** and that today are central parts of a modern jewelry manufacturing system.

### 3.1 AI-assisted design (generative CAD)

In 2026 a customer can bring a photo from Pinterest or a hand drawing, and AI tools generate in **seconds** a base 3D model that the designer refines. The process that used to take days now takes minutes. This **does not replace the designer**: it frees them from mechanical transcription so they can focus on what is creative and manufacturable. AI in design produces visualizations; professional CAD produces manufacturable models with tolerances; **both coexist**.

### 3.2 Predictive maintenance

The casting furnaces, presses, baths and polishing tubs of the jewelry workshop are expensive equipment that, when they fail, stop the line. AI, fed by IoT sensors, **predicts breakdowns before they occur**: detects vibration, temperature or electrical consumption patterns that announce a failure and schedules maintenance in a planned window instead of an emergency stop.

### 3.3 Automated regulatory compliance

LBMA for gold, Kimberley for diamonds, RJC for the ethical chain, digital VAT declarations (SII), electronic invoicing, AML registration for precious metals. AI does what would be unfeasible manually: **review supplier documentation, validate certificates, cross-reference batches with sold pieces, generate auditable traceability** in real time.

## 5. Real risks: what AI doesn't solve (and can make worse)

For the post to be useful and not triumphalist, it is worth naming the real risks:

- **Bias in training data.** If the model is trained with mislabeled defective pieces, it will perpetuate errors. Data quality rules.
- **Black box.** Some deep learning models detect defects but don't explain why. For a master jeweler used to understanding every decision, this can generate justified resistance.
- **Real implementation cost.** AI in production is not free: industrial cameras, dataset labeling (500-2,000 samples per defect type), ERP integration and team training. Payback exists, but requires initial investment.
- **False sense of security.** A model with 99% accuracy still fails 1 out of 100 times. In high-end jewelry, that 1% can be a very expensive piece.
- **Loss of craft.** If apprentices delegate to AI everything they used to learn by inspecting manually, tacit knowledge transmission is lost. Implementation must be designed so AI teaches, not hides. That is why a good [specialized jewelry ERP](/en) does not replace the craft: it augments it, keeping the human as the final decision-maker.

## 7. Conclusion: Deming is not obsolete. He is better served than ever.

Forty years after *Out of the Crisis*, the 14 points still describe with notable precision what separates a company that produces quality from one that produces systemic defects. What has changed is the **toolbox** available to implement them.

AI does not replace Deming. **It materializes him.** It makes executable principles that in 1986 were aspirational for many companies because the technology of the moment did not reach.

But —and this is the most important— poorly implemented AI **betrays** Deming. If used as surveillance to penalize errors instead of to correct the system, it generates fear (point 8). If its metrics are imposed as arbitrary quotas, it violates point 11. If it replaces deep training with superficial automation, it violates point 13.

The difference between a good implementation and a bad one is not in the power of the model. It is in whether management understands the 14 points.

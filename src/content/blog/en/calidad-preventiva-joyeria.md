If a stone is loose and it is found at Shipping, the piece has already been polished, set and plated. All that work went into a piece of jewelry that was wrong long before. And if the defect reaches the customer, you lose more than the stone: you lose the piece and the customer's trust.

Traditional quality control inspects at the end. **In-process quality control** measures within the route, right after the stage that can fail. It is the difference between finding the problem and stopping it.

---

## 1. Where jewelry defects start

In a jewelry workshop, defects do not appear at random. They almost always start in a handful of stages:

- **Casting:** this is where fineness and weight are born. An alloy at the limit, porosity or shrinkage show up here.
- **Setting:** loose stones, uneven prongs, a stone that does not sit right.
- **Polishing:** too much metal removed, or an uneven finish.
- **Finishing:** the final check of size, clasp, hallmark and appearance before the piece leaves.

With controls at those stages, the defect is caught while it is still cheap to fix.

## 2. A control point is a gate between two stages

The idea is simple: the key stages of the route have a **control point**. When the exit from that stage is recorded, the inspection is created automatically, with the lot, the sample, the recorded weight and who made the piece.

If the control blocks, **the piece cannot enter the next stage** until the inspection is accepted. And here is the rule that makes it workable in a workshop: **the exit is never blocked**. The operator can always finish their work; what waits is the next step.

Each inspection is closed with one of four decisions: accepted, accepted with a documented deviation, rework or rejection. If it is rejected, the replacement is created right from the inspection.

## 3. Who measures what

Not everything has to be measured by the supervisor:

- **Self-inspection:** whoever makes the piece checks it when they finish. It works very well in polishing: the polisher sees their own mistakes and production does not stop.
- **Inspector:** critical checks are done by the supervisor or a quality person, from their pending queue.

And work done outside goes into the same system. An outside specialist doing a single operation (setting, plating, engraving) or a subcontracted workshop making the whole piece goes through the same control point. Work coming back from outside is inspected on receipt.

## 4. What gets measured in jewelry

Generic quality control knows nothing about fineness or weight. A jewelry workshop needs a template with its own checks:

| Characteristic | How it is measured |
|---|---|
| Weight | 0.01 g scale |
| Metal fineness | XRF or touchstone (for example, 750–760‰ in 18K gold) |
| Size | Gauge |
| Stones secure | Yes / No |
| No porosity or cracks | 10x loupe |
| Finish | OK / Fair / Poor |
| Hallmark, clasp, color | Yes / No or list |

Some characteristics are **critical**. If fineness comes out at 745‰, the piece cannot simply be accepted: it is either rejected and replaced, reworked, or accepted with a signed deviation. Each defect is logged from a catalog (porosity, loose stone, soldering, out of size…) and, if needed, with a photo taken on a phone.

## 5. Orders of 1 to 5 pieces: jewelry is different

Industrial quality systems are designed for lots of hundreds of identical pieces: a sample is checked and statistics are applied to the lot. A jewelry workshop works the other way round: many orders a day, one to five pieces each.

That changes the rules:

- **No sampling:** the whole envelope is checked.
- **With several pieces, record the worst value.** If three rings weigh 4.12, 4.08 and 4.15 g, record 4.08 and put all three values in the notes.
- **If one in three fails,** create the replacement for the bad one; the good ones can move on with a documented deviation.
- **Size the supervisor's workload.** With 30 orders a day, three inspector controls mean 90 sheets a day. To start with, an inspector at setting and finishing, and self-inspection at casting and polishing, is usually enough.

## 6. From inspection to statistics

Every numeric measurement that is stored is useful for more than accepting or rejecting. With **statistical process control (SPC)** you see the trend before there are rejections:

- A low **Cpk** on casting fineness warns that the alloy is getting close to the limit before a piece comes out at 749‰.
- A weight that **drifts** in polishing warns that more metal than necessary is being removed.
- The **defect Pareto** shows which fault repeats most, at which stage, with which operator or at which outside workshop. With numbers, holding a supplier to account stops being an argument.

## 7. How to start without stopping the workshop

What works best is starting in **warn-only** mode: controls are created but do not block. After a week or two, the indicators show how long it takes to close each inspection and where things really fail, and then blocking is switched on at the stages that need it.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is in-process quality control in a jewelry workshop?

It means measuring quality within the manufacturing route, right after the stage that can fail, instead of inspecting only at the end. The defect is caught where it starts, and no more work goes into a piece that was already wrong.

### Does in-process quality control slow production down?

It does not have to. The exit from a stage can always be recorded; what is held, if the control blocks, is entry into the next stage until the inspection is accepted. You can start in warn-only mode.

### Is AQL sampling useful in a jewelry workshop?

Not much. AQL tables are designed for lots of hundreds or thousands of pieces; with orders of 1 to 5 pieces they call for inspecting almost the whole lot. The practical approach is 100% inspection of each envelope.

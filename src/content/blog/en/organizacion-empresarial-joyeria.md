# Organizational structure in jewelry businesses: Mintzberg's 5 configurations applied to the sector

> *Most of the problems that an ERP "cannot solve" in a jewelry business are not software problems. They are organizational structure problems that the system, by demanding traceability and permissions, exposes.*

When a jewelry business with its own workshop, one or several stores and, often, a wholesale arm decides to implement a specialized ERP like **RayTecno/RayGold**, the first findings are not usually technical. They are organizational. Suddenly questions appear that had gone years without a clear answer:

- Who authorizes a price change for a B2B customer?
- Does the store manager report to production or to commercial?
- Who decides which references are manufactured and which are bought?
- Does purchasing depend on manufacturing or have its own autonomy?

The ERP doesn't invent these questions: **it makes them visible**. And the correct answer depends on the **type of organizational structure** the company has chosen —consciously or by inertia— to adopt.

In this article we merge the classic frameworks of organizational theory (formal organization, line, functional, staff, departmentalization, committees, delegation) with the most current and recognized proposal in the field —**Henry Mintzberg's five configurations**— and ground them in the jewelry sector, showing how each structure conditions the implementation and real performance of an ERP.

---

## 1. Starting point: why structure precedes software

An **organizational structure**, according to Mintzberg's own definition, is *"the sum total of the ways in which work is divided into distinct tasks and then coordinated"*. Its purpose is to establish a system of roles that allows the team to work optimally toward common objectives.

For that structure to work, four basic requirements must be met:

- **Verifiable and achievable objectives** (quantitative to be precise, qualitative to be verifiable).
- **Clear definition of duties, rights and activities** of each person.
- **Delimitation of the authority area** of each position.
- **Defined information flows**: who receives what, when and from whom.

When these requirements are not covered, the organization is **informal**: things happen "because someone does them", not because there is a system. It's the most common model in medium-sized family jewelry businesses, and it works reasonably well… until the business grows. From around 10-15 people or the opening of a second channel (web, wholesale, second store), hidden costs explode: stock errors, duplicate orders, miscalculated margins, B2B customers receiving different rates depending on whom they talk to.

**Operational conclusion:** an ERP needs a **formal** organization to work well. Each registered action (a delivery note, a stock movement, an invoice, a price change) requires an identifiable author with defined permissions. If the organization is not formalized first, the ERP ends up configured with "everyone can do everything" and its main benefit is lost: traceability.

> **First step before any implementation:** draw the real organizational chart (not the ideal one) of the jewelry business. If it can't be drawn on a sheet, you have to formalize before computerizing.

## 2. Mintzberg's 5 configurations applied to jewelry

Mintzberg proposes that every organization is composed of five parts —strategic apex, operating core, middle line, technostructure and support staff— and that, depending on how they coordinate, it fits better in one of these five configurations. The effective organization is the one that achieves **coherence between its parts**, not the one that copies the fashion of the moment.

### 2.1 Simple Structure — the small entrepreneur's jewelry business

**Definition:** one or few administrators and a group of operators. Coordination by **direct supervision** from the apex. Minimal standardization, little middle line, highly centralized control. Young and small organizations operating in dynamic environments.

**Jewelry case:** the traditional jewelry business with the owner at the helm, two or three salespeople and, perhaps, a repair workshop. The owner decides on purchases, prices, hiring, repairs and difficult customer service. It is flexible, fast, close, but **does not scale**: when the owner is absent, the business slows down.

**Implication for the ERP:** this is the scenario where a [specialized jewelry ERP](/en) **gives its greatest returns**. Typical risk: buying more software than will be used. RayTecno allows starting with a functional core (sales, stock, invoicing) and growing modularly when the organization demands it.

### 2.2 Machine Bureaucracy — the jewelry business with chain of stores and serial production

**Definition:** coordination through **standardization of work**. Lots of technostructure (analysts who design processes), large support staff, marked hierarchy in the middle line, formal authority concentrated at the apex. Works well in simple and stable environments. Classic examples: automotive, insurance, McDonald's.

**Jewelry case:** jewelry chains with 20+ stores and serial production (wedding bands, standard chains, communion signet rings). Processes defined to the millimeter: each reference has its file, each store its operations manual, each employee their procedure. Efficient for volume, **rigid in the face of change**.

**Implication for the ERP:** this is the scenario where a specialized ERP **gives its greatest returns**. Standardization of multi-store rates, centralized stock control, uniform KPIs, process replication. RayTecno fits especially well in this profile with its multi-tenant architecture and its integrated manufacturing and distribution modules. Risk to watch: the main problem of machine bureaucracy is the **obsession with control**, which can paralyze commercial creativity in the store.

### 2.3 Professional Bureaucracy — the high goldsmithing and design jewelry business

**Definition:** coordination by **standardization of skills and knowledge**. Highly trained professionals at the operating core working with autonomy, little direct supervision, large support staff for routine tasks. Works in complex but stable environments. Classic examples: hospitals, universities, law firms.

**Jewelry case:** **fine jewelry** workshop or designer jewelry, where master jewelers, gemologists and designers are the critical asset. Quality depends on craft, not on process. The apex cannot "supervise" a master setter: it can only hire them, train them and give them resources.

**Implication for the ERP:** here the ERP **must not standardize the craft** (it would be counterproductive), but **traceability** around the craft: control of precious raw material by exact grams, photographic record of unique pieces, scrap management, detailed technical files. RayTecno allows defining manufacturing orders with flexibility for unique pieces, maintaining complete traceability of gold, gems and workshop hours.

### 2.4 Divisionalized Form — the multi-brand or multi-line jewelry group

**Definition:** **it is not a complete structure, but a partial one**, superimposed on others. Several semi-autonomous units (divisions) coordinated by a central administration that measures their performance. Internally each division tends to operate as a machine bureaucracy. Divisionalization **is not the same as decentralization**: strategic decisions remain centralized, what is delegated is operations.

**Jewelry case:** business group with several brands (bridal jewelry + casual costume jewelry + technical watchmaking), or jewelry business with **divisions by channel** (retail / wholesale / online). Each division has its objectives, its margins, its responsible person, but all report to a general management that measures performance with comparable KPIs.

**Implication for the ERP:** this is the scenario that best exploits RayTecno's **multi-tenant** architecture. Each division can operate with its own logical database, its rates, its catalogs and its permissions, while general management has a consolidated view for comparative analysis. It's the typical configuration of customers managing several tenants (jewelry businesses and sub-brands) on the same platform.

### 2.5 Adhocracy — the special projects and bespoke jewelry workshop

**Definition:** the most flexible and complex. Coordination by **mutual adjustment** between experts, through informal communication. Matrix structure, project teams, authority based on competence and not on hierarchy. Lots of innovation, **at the expense of efficiency**.

Mintzberg distinguishes two types:

- **Operating adhocracy:** works on customer order, treating each project as unique.
- **Administrative adhocracy:** the design/innovation part works as adhocracy; the production part is separated to avoid contaminating it.

**Jewelry case:** **bespoke jewelry and unique pieces** workshop (custom engagement rings, commemorative jewels, restoration of historical pieces). Each piece is a project, with a team formed ad hoc according to complexity: 3D designer, gemologist, setter, polisher.

**Implication for the ERP:** adhocracy is the **greatest challenge** for any ERP. It requires extreme flexibility in manufacturing orders, customized quotes, validation milestones with the customer and photographic traceability of the process. The solution is **modular**: the ERP provides the framework (customer, quote, materials, costs) and lets the creative flow breathe on top.

## 3. Five transversal concepts that the ERP makes operational

Regardless of which configuration predominates, there are concepts that every jewelry business must resolve and that the ERP makes operational.

### 3.1 Span of control

A director should have **only the subordinates they can manage efficiently**. Reduced span of control → more control and supervision, but more cost and bureaucracy. Wide span → demands delegating and training well, but generates bottlenecks if plans are not clear.

**In ERP practice:** role permissions and approval flows (a discount above X% requires validation from the store manager; one above Y%, from the commercial director) are the technical translation of span of control.

### 3.2 Delegation of authority

Work and authority are delegated, **but never the final responsibility**. Good delegation requires:

- Defining expected results.
- Assigning specific tasks.
- Granting necessary authority.
- Establishing controls, not interference.

**In ERP practice:** a salesperson with permission to create delivery notes but not to modify rates, a manager who can approve returns up to a certain amount, a purchasing manager who can issue orders to suppliers but requires approval to exceed a limit. All of that is delegation formalized in the system.

### 3.3 Line vs Staff

- **Line:** directly responsible for objectives (store, workshop, sales).
- **Staff:** advise and support (administration, management control, marketing, IT).

Staff **suggests, does not command**. Confusing both roles is one of the most common sources of friction in growing jewelry businesses.

**In ERP practice:** "consultation" profiles (who see data but do not operate) and "execution" profiles (who operate in their area) reflect the line/staff difference. A financial controller sees margins and costs but does not invoice; a store manager invoices but does not modify the chart of accounts.

### 3.4 Departmentalization

The way activities are grouped. The most relevant in jewelry:

- **By functions** (purchases, manufacturing, sales, administration): suitable for stable jewelry businesses with few lines.
- **By products** (gold, silver, fine jewelry, costume jewelry): when each line needs its own focus.
- **By geographical zones**: when there are several stores or commercial regions.
- **By customers** (retail vs B2B vs online): when the customer is the determining factor.
- **Matrix** (functions × products or × projects crossover): for special orders and complex projects.

**In ERP practice:** departmentalization translates into how cost centers, warehouses, invoicing series and analytical dimensions are organized. Bad accounting departmentalization drags on for years in reports.

### 3.5 Committees and work teams

A **committee** is a group assigned a matter (decision, study, recommendation). It is useful for transversal coordination, but expensive and slow if abused. Good committees have a clear mission, defined authority, appropriate members and measurable results.

Key difference with a **team**: in the group leadership is hierarchical and responsibility individual; in the team leadership is shared and responsibility joint.

**In ERP practice:** catalog review committees, monthly closing committees or investment validation committees are more efficient when supported by ERP dashboards, not on improvised spreadsheets. Committee quality depends on the quality of the information in front of it.

## 5. Conclusion: structure first, ERP later (or both at once, but never the other way around)

Mintzberg sums it up with a powerful idea: **the effective organization is the one that achieves coherence between its parts**. When that coherence fails, no software supplies it. But when it exists, a specialized ERP becomes **the lever that multiplies the structure's performance**.

The questions opening this article —who authorizes, who decides, who reports— are not questions that an ERP answers. They are questions that **management must answer first**, and that an ERP then translates into permissions, flows and traceability.

# The Factory Map: see your jewelry workshop's entire production at a single glance

In the [first article of this series](https://www.raytecno.es/en/blog/marcajes-produccion-joyeria) we talked about production tracking: how to get the data on what happens in the workshop to be born at the workstation, at the exact moment, instead of reconstructing it afterward with paper sheets. We solved the problem of *capturing* the information.

But capturing the data is only half the road. The other half is **being able to see it**. Because there's little point in having every movement of every batch recorded to the millisecond if, when the manager wants to know how production is going, they have to dive into tables, cross-reference lists or —most commonly— go down to the workshop to ask.

That's where the **Factory Map** comes in: the view that turns all those trackings into a **live image of the workshop**. A single screen where you see, at a glance, what's happening right now in each production phase. It's possibly the tool that most changes the way a workshop is run, because it replaces the question *"how are we doing?"* with an answer that's always in sight.

## The problem: production is invisible until you go and look

A jewelry workshop is a long, fragmented process. A piece can pass through casting, die-making, laser, filing, barrels, polishing, setting, plating, finishing and dispatch. In each of those phases there are pieces from different orders, from different customers, in different states. And all that movement happens spread across the workshop's physical space, on trays and in envelopes that go from one bench to another.

The result is that **production is invisible**. Not because information doesn't exist, but because it's scattered. To know the real status you have to reconstruct it mentally: go bench by bench, ask each operator, add up what each one has in front of them. And by the time that count is done, it has already changed.

This generates a series of situations anyone who runs a workshop will recognize:

- **The constant question "where is so-and-so's order?"** has no immediate answer. You have to go find it, and that interrupts whoever asks and whoever has to answer.
- **You don't know where work is piling up.** A phase can be saturated with pieces waiting while another sits idle, and nobody notices until the jam has already caused a delay.
- **The manager lives "putting out fires."** Since they don't see the whole, they react to problems when they blow up, instead of anticipating them by seeing them coming.
- **Information depends on people.** If the foreman who "has it all in his head" is off sick or on holiday, control of the workshop suffers.

The underlying problem is always the same: **there's no global, real-time view of the workshop.** And without that view, running production is driving while looking only in the rear-view mirror.

## The solution: a screen that is the workshop

The Factory Map solves this in the most direct way possible: **it represents the entire workshop on a single screen**, organized by production phases, and shows in each one what's at stake at this moment.

The visual logic is deliberately intuitive, because it has to be understood in a second, without a manual. For each phase of the flow —casting, filing, barrels, polishing, setting, dispatch and all the rest— the map clearly distinguishes between what's **waiting** (pieces that have reached that phase but aren't yet being worked on) and what's **being worked on** right now. At a single glance, the manager sees where production flows and, above all, **where it's piling up**.

Each manufacturing line is identified with a **color**, so it's possible to visually follow a specific item or order throughout its entire journey through the workshop. You don't have to read codes or search lists: you follow the color. And next to each element appears the detail of **which item it is and which customer it's for**, so the question "where is so-and-so's order?" is answered by looking at the screen.

## In real time: the map breathes with the workshop

What makes the Factory Map a control tool and not just a nice diagram is that **it's alive**. Each tracking an operator makes on the shop floor —those entry and exit trackings from the first article— is reflected on the map **instantly**.

When an operator finishes a batch in polishing and marks the exit, the piece "moves" on the map at that very moment, passing to the next phase. The manager doesn't have to refresh anything or request a report: what they see on screen *is* the workshop, now. This direct connection between the operator's gesture and the global image is what closes the circle: the data is born at the workstation (tracking) and becomes visible to everyone within a second (map).

This completely changes the dynamics of management. The foreman can have the Factory Map open on a screen and, without moving, know at all times what's being worked on, what's waiting and how much each plan has progressed. Control stops depending on going down to the workshop and stops depending on one specific person "having it in their head."

## Filter to find: from the whole workshop to the specific order

Seeing the whole workshop is very useful for the production manager. But often the question is more specific: *I want to see just this plan*, *I want to see what I have for this customer*, *I want to follow this specific order*. The Factory Map allows you to **filter the view by plan, by order or by customer**, so the same map serves both for the overall view and for specific follow-up.

This has direct commercial value: when a customer calls asking about their order, instead of a "let me check and call you back," you can filter by their name and tell them right away which phase it's in and how much is left. The sense of control that conveys to the customer is priceless.

In addition, the map shows the **plan's overall progress** as a percentage, an immediate reading of how much has been completed and how much remains, without having to go into any detail.

## Much more than looking: the plan's command center

The Factory Map isn't just a screen to observe; it's the point from which you access everything related to a plan. From the map itself, the manager can:

- **Consult the plan's complete analysis**, with its cycle times, bottleneck and deviations —which will be the topic of the series' third article.
- **See the delivery traffic light**: a color indicator that says at a glance whether the plan is on time (green), at risk (amber) or late (red), and when clicked explains *why*, with the critical line, the bottleneck and the missing materials.
- **Access the plan's images**, to have in front of you what's being manufactured.

In other words, the Factory Map works as the **command center** of production: the place you go to know how everything is doing and from which you drill down into whatever is needed.

## What a jewelry business gains with total visibility

Going from invisible production to production you see on a screen has very tangible benefits:

- **Immediate answers.** "Where is the order?" stops being an expedition through the workshop and becomes a glance at the screen.
- **Bottlenecks detected in time.** When a phase starts piling up pieces waiting, you see it on the map before it becomes a delay. You act by anticipating, not by firefighting.
- **Control independent of people.** The workshop's status is in the system and visible to everyone, not in one person's head. Production doesn't stop because someone is absent.
- **Better customer service.** Being able to say instantly which phase an order is in and how much is left conveys professionalism and builds trust.
- **Faster, better day-to-day decisions.** With the whole in view, reorganizing priorities, reassigning work or moving up an urgent order is done with judgment, not blindly.

## In summary

If production tracking solves the *what happened and when*, the Factory Map solves the *what's happening right now and where*. It turns a scattered, invisible process into a single, live image that's understandable at a glance.

For a jewelry workshop —where many orders, many phases and much value on each tray coexist— having that global real-time view isn't an ornament: it's the difference between running production and chasing it.

In many jewelry workshops, the time for each operation is an estimate someone made years ago. And a lot depends on that number: the **labor cost** of each piece and, with it, the price; the **capacity** of each section; the **planning** of orders; and the only way to know whether an improvement actually improved anything.

A time study replaces the estimate with a time that is **measured, rated and documented**: who measured it, when and how.

---

## 1. From observed time to standard time

What the stopwatch shows is not yet the time to use. The calculation goes through three steps:

| Step | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Mean observed time (MOT)** | The average of the valid timed cycles. |
| **Normal time (NT)** | MOT adjusted for the worker's pace and the operation's difficulty. |
| **Standard time (ST)** | Normal time plus allowances for fatigue and personal needs. This is what goes into the route. |

As a formula: **ST = MOT × PR × (DF + 1) / 100 × (ALW + 1)**.

**An example with soldering.** The mean observed time is 95 seconds. The time-study observer rated the worker 10% faster than normal (PR = 110). The operation has a difficulty of 0.11 (wrist and fingers, close visual attention, careful handling). That gives a normal time of 95 × 1.221 = 116 seconds. With allowances of 0.14 (personal needs, basic fatigue, visual strain and posture), the standard time comes to **132 seconds, about 2 minutes 12 seconds per piece**.

## 2. Why you need 10 to 15 cycles

A single timing is not a standard time: it is an observation. In manual jewelry work the same operation varies from one piece to the next because of the wax, the metal, the setting, fatigue, the time of day or the person.

The standard work-measurement method calculates how many observations are needed for an error of ±5% at 95% confidence. With the first five readings:

- A **very regular** operation, machine-paced or fixed (60, 62, 58, 65 and 61 seconds), needs about **3 cycles**.
- A **typical manual jewelry** operation (60, 70, 52, 66 and 58 seconds) needs about **17**.

That is why it makes sense to set a rule of **at least 10 cycles, ideally 15**, per item:

- Below 10 cycles, the time is a guide but does not go into the route.
- Cycles are spread across **several workers, different days, mornings and afternoons**, so the time reflects reality rather than one person or one day.
- A cycle with an abnormal incident (a defective piece, a long interruption) is **excluded**: it stays on record but does not count in the average.
- If the calculation always uses the **15 most recent cycles**, old cycles drop out as the method improves and the standard updates itself.

## 3. Rating, difficulty and allowances

These are the three factors that turn a stopwatch reading into a fair time.

**Performance rating (PR).** For each operation the observer rates the pace at which the person is working, usually on the 100-point scale: 100 is normal pace, from 85 (slow) to 125 (very fast). That way the time does not depend on who did it.

**Difficulty factor (DF).** Each operation gets a grade in several categories (body part used, use of both hands, eye-hand coordination, care in handling) and DF is the sum. In jewelry, **close visual attention** and precision to tenths of a millimeter weigh heavily.

**Allowances (ALW).** They compensate for fatigue and personal needs: standing, awkward posture, the visual strain of setting or soldering, noise, monotony. And here there is an important decision: **allowances depend on the workstation, not on the person**.

## 4. The same time, whoever does the job

The ILO reference allowance table, the historical basis of almost every time study, gave some different values for men and women. In RayGold we do not make that distinction: each allowance has a single percentage, the higher of the two, so that **nobody is disadvantaged** and the same operation has the same standard time whoever does it.

This is consistent with the principle of equal pay for work of equal value. And each company can adjust the percentages to its own ergonomic assessment.

## 5. Stoppages are not part of the time

Breakdowns, maintenance, breaks or a meeting are recorded as **stoppages**. If one happens in the middle of an operation, the operation is paused and its time deducted: only net working time counts. Stoppages are analyzed separately, because they say a lot about the workshop, but they do not inflate the standard.

And when a method changes (a new tool, a different order, another way of preparing the wax tree), the only way to prove the improvement is a new study compared with the previous one.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many timings are needed to set a standard time?

Between 10 and 15 cycles per item, spread across several operators, days and shifts. In manual jewelry work a single measurement varies too much: below 10 cycles the time is a guide, but it should not go into the route.

### What is the difference between observed, normal and standard time?

Observed time is the average of what was timed. Normal time adjusts for the worker's pace and the difficulty of the operation. Standard time adds allowances for fatigue and personal needs, and is the one used for costing and planning.

### Do allowances depend on the person?

No, they depend on the workstation: posture, standing, visual strain, noise, monotony. They are the same for anyone who does the operation.

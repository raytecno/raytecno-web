A new piece of jewelry is not born in the workshop. Before that there is a sketch, a carved wax or a 3D-printed resin, a rubber mold and a first pilot cast in metal. At every step the weight, the stones and the hours change, and the cost with them. If nobody tracks it, the first time anyone knows whether the piece pays is when it is already being sold.

That earlier work has a name: **product engineering**. And it answers three questions: **is it profitable, is it ready to make, and what has changed and why?**

---

## 1. Seven stages, from idea to released piece

Organizing development in stages stops a model from reaching production half-finished:

1. **Sketch.** The idea: family, collection, customer if it is a commission, target price and unit forecast.
2. **Prototype.** Physical test models: carved wax, 3D-printed resin, rubber mold. Each with its operator, hours and spend.
3. **Pilot.** The first piece cast in metal. It sets the **real weight**.
4. **Viability.** The full cost is valued with the bill of materials and the route, and the margin decision is made.
5. **Readiness.** The checks are closed: pilot quality, commercial approval, stones, photos, packaging.
6. **Released.** The final item is created, ready for production.
7. **Rejected.** Always with a reason.

What matters is that no stage can be skipped: no pilot without a prototype, no viability without a bill of materials, no release without a valued cost.

## 2. From 3D file to grams

The design is done in whatever CAD program each company uses. What matters for the product study is the **STL file**: it gives the piece's volume and, with the metal's density, the estimated weight.

| Metal | Density | Weight of the sample hoop |
|---|---|---|
| 18K yellow gold | 15.5 g/cm³ | 2.44 g |
| 18K rose gold | 15.1 g/cm³ | 2.38 g |
| 925 silver | 10.36 g/cm³ | 1.63 g |
| 950 platinum | 20.3 g/cm³ | 3.20 g |

*18 mm hoop earring with 2 mm wire: 0.1574 cm³.*

With the same file, the piece is compared in different metals before anything is cast. And when the pilot arrives, its real weight replaces the estimate.

## 3. Cost, stage by stage

The cost of a new piece is made up of blocks that can be calculated from the very beginning:

- **Metal:** weight × (1 + casting loss) × fineness × fine metal price.
- **Stones, findings and packaging:** from the piece's bill of materials.
- **Labor:** hours per piece for each operation × section rate.
- **Subcontracting:** outside casting, plating, setting done elsewhere.
- **Development:** prototype spend spread across the forecast units.

**An example.** A pair of 22 mm hammered hoops in 18K gold with a 3.50 g pilot: metal comes to 261.84 (with 5% casting loss, 0.750 fineness and gold at 95 per gram), hinges 5.60, labor 15.00, outside casting 3.00, the case 3.90 and development 0.44 (174 in prototypes over 400 units). **Total cost: 289.78. Metal is 90%.**

With a target price of 470, the margin is 38.3%. If the target is 40%, the piece is **amber**: 1.7 points short. To get there it would have to sell above 482.97. And with the CAD estimated weight (3.41 g) the margin came out at 39.8%: **nine hundredths of a gram** move the margin by a point and a half.

That is why it pays to keep every valuation exactly as it was, with that day's metal price, and to compare versions side by side instead of recalculating everything each time.

## 4. The margin traffic light

A simple traffic light makes the decision quick:

- **Green:** the margin meets the target.
- **Amber:** up to 5 points below. Price, weight or design can be adjusted.
- **Red:** more than 5 points below. It needs rethinking.

The **minimum price** (cost ÷ (1 − target margin)) says exactly below which price the target is missed.

## 5. Readiness: what must be in place before production

Before releasing a model there is a checklist. Some checks are ticked by the system as soon as they are met: design uploaded, bill of materials, route, approved prototype and pilot, real weight and current cost. Others are ticked by the person responsible: commercial approval of price and margin, and the quality review of the pilot (porosity, setting…). And some are optional: stones confirmed with the supplier, catalog photos, packaging.

Once every mandatory check is done, the model is released: the final item is created with its bill of materials, stages and stock, and the version is frozen.

## 6. Versions and change orders

A product can have several versions: another metal, another thickness, other stones. Each new version carries its reason and is compared with the others by cost.

A released version is never edited. To change a product that is already in production, a **change order** is opened with its reason and impact, and once approved a new version is created that goes through the stages again. That way you always know what was made, at what cost and why it changed.

## 7. Two documents: one for the customer, one for the workshop

The same study produces two documents:

- The **customer sheet**: image, specifications, composition without amounts, indicative price and sign-off boxes. No costs or margins.
- The **internal technical dossier**: the whole study, with the cost breakdown, bill of materials, route, prototypes, readiness and valuation history.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do you calculate the weight of a piece before casting it?

From the STL file's volume and the metal's density. An 18 mm hoop earring with 2 mm wire has 0.1574 cm³: about 2.44 g in 18K yellow gold (15.5 g/cm³) and 1.63 g in 925 silver (10.36 g/cm³). The cast pilot then gives the real weight.

### What goes into the cost of a new piece?

Metal (weight with casting loss, fineness and metal price), stones and findings, labor from the route, subcontracted work, packaging and development: prototype spend spread across the forecast units.

### What is product readiness?

The checklist that must be complete before production: design uploaded, bill of materials, route, approved prototype and pilot, real weight, current cost, commercial approval and quality review.

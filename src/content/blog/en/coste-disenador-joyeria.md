In many jewelry businesses, the designer's salary goes entirely to one place: either overhead or manufacturing. Both options are convenient and both allocate the cost badly. Because the designer actually does **two different jobs**, and each one is paid for in a different part of the price.

---

## 1. Two jobs, two parts of the price

| | Technical design | Collection design |
|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | 3D models, masters for rubber molds, technical sheets, prototypes | New collections, trends, sales samples |
| **Type of cost** | Manufacturing cost: without this work the piece cannot be made | Period expense: it is product development and marketing, not making a piece |
| **Where it is paid** | In the cost of every piece that goes through the workshop, via hourly rates | In the markup on the price of every piece |

## 2. Why not put it all in one place

**If it were all period expense,** the cost would be spread over every piece equally, whether they use it or not. A catalog chain made the same way for years would pay for the designer as much as a ring from a new collection.

**If it were all manufacturing,** workshop pieces would also pay for the work of creating collections, which has nothing to do with making them. Manufacturing cost would be inflated, and so would hourly rates.

**Split according to time,** each piece pays what genuinely belongs to it. This is how large management systems handle it: the same person can be split between manufacturing and overhead.

## 3. An example

If the designer's annual cost to the company is 60,000 and their time is split 60% technical design and 40% collections:

| Part | % | Amount | Goes to |
|---|---|---|---|
| Technical design | 60% | 36,000 | Manufacturing cost of the pieces |
| Collection design | 40% | 24,000 | Price markup |
| **Total** | **100%** | **60,000** | Nothing lost, nothing counted twice |

The 36,000 goes into the annual cost of the design or model-making section and, divided by its hours, into its **hourly rate**. So every piece that goes through that section pays its share according to the time it needs. The 24,000 is added to the overhead covered by the **markup** on the selling price.

## 4. Two things to confirm

For the split to work, two pieces of information are needed:

1. **How their time is really split.** 60/40 is an example: it could be 70/30 or 50/50. If it changes, only the percentages change. If in doubt, it can be measured for a few weeks.
2. **Which account their payroll is booked to.** To make sure their cost is not counted twice: once through payroll in the hourly rates and again through the accounts in overhead.

## 5. The same applies to other roles

The designer is the clearest case, but not the only one. A supervisor who spends half the day at the bench and half organizing, or a purchasing person who also prepares casting orders, has the same problem. The rule is the same: **the cost goes where the time goes**.

In RayGold, the split is done in the [workshop's hourly rates](/en/modules/manufacturing): each section adds up its annual cost, including its share of each person, and divides it by its hours. That way the costing of every piece, and the study of every [new model before it is made](/en/modules/product-engineering), starts from a cost that can be explained.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the designer's salary a manufacturing cost or overhead?

It depends on the work. Technical design (3D models, masters for rubber molds, technical sheets, prototypes) is needed to make the piece and goes into manufacturing cost. Collection design (trends, sales samples) is product development and marketing and goes into period expense.

### How is the designer's cost split?

According to the time spent on each job. If 60% is technical design and 40% collections, 60% of their annual cost goes into the workshop's hourly rates and 40% into the markup on the price of every piece.

### Why not put it all in overhead?

Because it is then spread over every piece equally, whether they use it or not. A catalog piece that has been made the same way for years ends up paying for the design work on new collections.
